Apply psychological principles to marketing, copy, and design. Based on Cialdini's principles, cognitive bias research from Kahneman and Tversky, and conversion optimization data from 500+ A/B test analyses.

## The 7 Universal Persuasion Principles (Cialdini + expanded)

| Principle | Description | Application |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| Reciprocity | People feel obliged to give back what they receive | Free trials, valuable content upfront, bonuses, samples |
| Scarcity | People want what is limited or hard to get | Limited time, limited stock, exclusive access, early bird |
| Authority | People trust experts and credible sources | Certifications, awards, media features, data citations, expert endorsements |
| Consistency | People stick with their past commitments and stated positions | Progressive commitments (micro-yes → macro-yes), checkboxes, opt-ins |
| Liking | People say yes to people they like | Relatable brand voice, similarity, shared values, compliments |
| Social Proof | People follow the actions of others | Testimonials, user count, case studies, reviews, "join 10,000+" |
| Unity | People trust and favor those in their "tribe" | Community, shared identity, membership language, "we"/"us" framing |

## Cognitive Biases for Marketing

| Bias | How it works | Marketing use |
|------|-------------|---------------|
| Anchoring | First number encountered sets the reference point | Show higher price first, then your price — makes it feel like a deal |
| Decoy effect | An unattractive third option makes the target option look better | 3 pricing tiers: middle is your real offer |
| Loss aversion | Losses hurt ~2x more than equivalent gains feel good | "You're missing out on X" over "You'll gain X" |
| Framing | Context changes perception dramatically | "90% success rate" vs "10% failure rate" |
| Endowment | People overvalue what they already have or have customized | Free trials (users don't want to lose access), personalization |
| Paradox of choice | More options = less action + lower satisfaction | Limit choices to 3-4 per decision point |
| Peak-end rule | People judge experiences by the peak moment and the ending | Make the best interaction great, end every experience on a high note |
| Confirmation bias | People seek info that confirms existing beliefs | Frame your message to align with what they already believe |
| Halo effect | One positive trait colors perception of everything else | Beautiful design → trust in reliability. Strong brand → belief in quality. |
| Zeigarnik effect | Interrupted tasks are remembered better than completed ones | Progress bars, partially filled forms, cliffhanger subject lines |
| Social identity | People define themselves by group membership | "For designers, by designers." "Join 50,000+ developers." |
| IKEA effect | People value what they helped create | User-generated content, customization, co-creation campaigns |
| Default bias | People stick with pre-selected options | Opt-out (default) converts much higher than opt-in |
| Sunk cost | People continue investing in something because they already invested | Points programs, tiered memberships, progress toward goal |

## Conversion Drivers

| Driver | Psychology | Implementation |
|--------|------------|----------------|
| Specificity | Concrete claims are more believable and trustworthy | "3.2x ROI" not "better results". "22 min" not "fast". |
| Social proof cascade | Seeing others choose triggers herd behavior | "1,247 people bought this today" + live purchase notifications |
| Risk reversal | Removing downside removes the primary objection | Free returns, money-back guarantee, "cancel anytime" |
| Scarcity (genuine) | Limited availability triggers urgency | "12 spots left" — must be real, verifiable |
| Personalization | Relevant messages feel like they're for "me" | Use name, location, past behavior, segment-specific offers |
| Progress effect | Seeing progress motivates completion | "Step 2 of 3", "80% complete", progress bars |
| Curiosity gap | Incomplete information compels us to learn more | Subject lines that hint but don't reveal, cliffhangers |
| Commitment ladder | Small commitments lead naturally to larger ones | Free account → try feature → see results → upgrade |
| Exclusion/scarcity | Closed groups feel more valuable | "Waitlist", "invite only", "beta access", "members only" |
| Reciprocity sequence | Give-then-ask converts higher than ask-only | Free guide → email course → case study → offer |
| Now vs later | Immediate benefit beats delayed benefit | "Get started in 5 minutes" vs "Start your journey" |
| By-now framing | Past tense makes decision feel inevitable | "Join 10,000+ customers who transformed their workflow" |
| Vanity metrics recognized | "For people like you" beats "for everyone" | "Built for revenue operations teams at scaling B2B companies" |
| Implementation prompts | Specific next steps remove friction | "Enter your email and we'll send the template" not "Sign up" |

## Copy Psychology Patterns

| Pattern | Effect | Example |
|---------|--------|---------|
| "You" vs "we" | You-focused copy is 2x more engaging | "You'll save 10 hours/week" not "We help you save time" |
| Present tense | Feels immediate and real | "Slack transforms how your team communicates" |
| Active voice | Stronger, more confident | "We cut your cloud costs by 40%" |
| Rhetorical questions | Engages the reader's internal dialogue | "What if you could double your output?" |
| Contrast | Highlights the difference | "Before: 20 hours. After: 2 hours." |
| Specific numbers | More believable than round numbers | "$1,497" not "$1,500". "93%" not "over 90%". |
| Sensory words | Activates emotional centers in the brain | "Feels silky", "sounds crisp", "cuts like..." |
| Short sentences | Creates rhythm and emphasis | "Fast. Reliable. Affordable. Pick two. We picked three." |

## Visual Psychology Patterns

| Pattern | Why it works | Application |
|---------|-------------|-------------|
| F-pattern layout | Matches natural reading behavior | Most important content top-left, CTAs on right |
| Gaze direction | People look where faces look | Product page faces pointing toward CTA or text |
| Color contrast | High contrast draws attention first | CTA button color: complementary to brand palette |
| White space | Reduces cognitive load, signals premium | Generous padding around CTAs and key messages |
| Visual hierarchy | Size + position = importance | Headline is the largest element on the page |
| Motion cues | Movement captures peripheral vision | Subtle hover animations, loading indicators |
| Closure | People complete incomplete shapes | Minimalist icons, negative space logos |

## Anti-Patterns

| Mistake | Consequence | Fix |
|---------|-------------|-----|
| Fake scarcity | Destroys trust permanently. Users remember. | Only use genuine scarcity with verifiable limits. |
| Too many choices | Decision paralysis, lower conversion | Limit to 3-4 options maximum per decision |
| No social proof | Low credibility, skeptical visitors | Add testimonials, user counts, endorsements near every claim |
| Overwhelming claims | Triggers skepticism, increases bounce | Back every claim with specific data and sources |
| Ignoring objections | Drop-off at the decision point | Address objections proactively before the reader thinks of them |
| Anchoring on weak frame | Wrong comparison makes your price look high | Frame against the higher-cost alternative, not the cheapest |
| Overusing urgency | Audience becomes desensitized | Use urgency only for genuinely time-limited offers |
| One-size-fits-all messaging | Resonates with no one deeply | Segment. Write to one person per segment. |
| No commitment ladder | Asks for too much too soon | Micro-commitments first: scroll → read → click → opt-in → buy |
| Dark patterns | Short-term gain, long-term brand damage | Never trick users. Never hide unsubscribe. Never pre-check opt-in. |
